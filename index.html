Cart-Level Decisions
Problem Audience Journey Direction Design Validation
Product opportunity · Discount grocery e-commerce

From product-level choices to cart-level decisions.

Shopping apps help shoppers compare and choose individual products. But once those choices come together in the cart, they get little help lowering the total or getting better value.

This case study explores the gap between the support available while choosing each product and the decisions shoppers face after seeing the completed cart.

01 · Framing the problem

Shoppers get strong support while choosing individual products, but little help improving the cart once those choices come together.

Shopping apps help shoppers search, compare prices and pack sizes, review price per unit, and choose individual products.

Once those products form a cart, the experience mainly presents the selected items, the total, and checkout.

If shoppers want to lower the total or get better value, they often have to return to earlier shopping steps and repeat work they have already completed.

Opportunity question
How might we help shoppers improve the cart without restarting the shopping journey?
02 · Audience and impact

The need becomes most visible when budget-conscious shoppers have built the cart and seen the total.

Working audience

A budget-conscious grocery shopper who builds a regular household cart and checks the total before checkout.

At that point, they may want to lower the total, get more for a similar spend, or choose a slightly higher-priced option that offers better value per unit.

Why it matters to the shopper

  • They have to return to search and repeat comparisons after already building the cart.
  • Relevant product details and trade-offs are spread across several pages.
  • They may need to calculate manually how each alternative would affect the total.
  • They may remove a product they still need because suitable alternatives are difficult to find.

Why it may matter to the business

  • Repeating search, comparison, and calculation may create more hesitation before checkout.
  • The order may be delayed or left incomplete.
  • Lower-priced or private-label alternatives may remain undiscovered even when they offer a clear shopper benefit.
Business hypothesis · unproven

These outcomes would require behavioral data and live testing.

03 · Current journey gap

The main friction appears after shoppers have selected their products, added them to the cart, and seen the total.

Where existing patterns stop

Promotions and recommendations

They help withDiscovering products and adding more items during shopping.
They do not help withReconsidering products already in the cart when the shopper wants to reduce the total or improve value.

Product comparison and unit pricing

They help withComparing products during an individual product decision.
They do not help withComparing suitable alternatives while keeping the completed cart in context.

Cart total and savings summary

They help withShowing the current total and applied discounts.
They do not help withIdentifying which products could be changed and showing how each change would affect the total.

Out-of-stock replacements

They help withReplacing a product that cannot be fulfilled.
They do not help withOffering optional alternatives based on price, pack size, private label, or price per unit.
While shopping
“Which product should I choose?”
In the cart
“What could I change to lower the total or get better value?”
Journey insight

The gap is not missing product information. The existing information is no longer organized around the completed cart when the shopper needs to lower the total or improve value.

04 · Choosing the first direction

I explored two directions for supporting decisions at the cart level.

The first changes how shoppers plan from the start. The second supports them after the cart is already built.

Direction 1 Budget-First Shopping Future direction

Shoppers would set a total budget and category limits before or during shopping.

What it could offer
  • Show the remaining total and category budgets while shopping.
  • Warn shoppers when spending approaches a limit.
  • Suggest alternatives that support the limits they set.
Why it comes later
  • It introduces a new planning pattern from the start of the journey.
  • It asks shoppers to manage limits while they shop.
  • It requires trust in the recommendation system before that trust has been earned.
Direction 2 Smart Basket Optimizer Selected

An optional tool inside the cart that shows a small number of transparent alternatives.

Same size, lower price Best price per unit Store brand (private label), lower price
Why it comes first
  • The Basket Optimizer addresses this friction inside the cart, where the shopper first sees the combined effect of their choices.
  • It fits the current journey without teaching a new shopping pattern.
  • Shoppers choose when to use it and confirm every change.
  • It can begin with limited categories and clear comparison rules.
What it needs to prove
  • The alternatives feel genuinely comparable.
  • The benefit of each option is easy to understand.
  • Using the feature requires less effort than manually finding and comparing alternatives.
  • The recommendations feel useful rather than commercially biased.

05 · Designing the experience

Compare alternatives and update the cart without returning to earlier shopping steps.

Smart Basket Optimizer appears as an optional action inside the cart. Shoppers can leave the Basket Optimizer and return to checkout at any time.

The first version focuses on categories where price, pack size, price per unit, and availability support a fair comparison.

Key design decisions
DecisionShow only fair comparisons

Do not show an alternative unless the product type, intended use, size, price, and available data support a reasonable comparison.

DecisionExplain the benefit

Use specific labels such as “Same size, lower price” instead of vague labels such as “Recommended.”

DecisionSeparate immediate saving from unit value

Use “save” only when the current cart total decreases. Show better price per unit as a different benefit.

DecisionKeep the current item visible

Let shoppers compare both products using the same information without remembering details or reopening product pages.

DecisionKeep every change reversible

Require confirmation before replacement and provide undo from the updated cart.

Focused MVP

Limited categories, reliable catalog data, a small number of transparent alternatives, preview of the new total, confirmation, and undo.

06 · Validation and next steps

Validate the need, then compare the current journey with the Basket Optimizer.

Assumption 1

The need is frequent enough to address

Shoppers regularly want to lower the total, get more for a similar spend, or choose a slightly higher-priced option that offers better value per unit.

Assumption 2

The alternatives earn trust

Shoppers understand why each option appears and consider it a reasonable replacement.

Assumption 3

The Basket Optimizer reduces effort

Reviewing alternatives inside the cart requires less searching, comparison, and calculation than finding them manually.

How I would test the direction

Validation approach

Step 1 — Confirm the need

Use product data and recent-shopping interviews to understand what shoppers do after seeing the cart total, how often they reconsider products, and which value trade-offs matter to them.

Step 2 — Compare the two journeys

Give shoppers the same completed cart and ask them to adjust it in two ways: by returning to search and by using Smart Basket Optimizer.

What I would evaluate

Time and effort
Does the Basket Optimizer reduce the time and effort spent searching, comparing, and calculating?
Understanding
Do shoppers understand what Optimize basket will do and why each alternative appears?
Value choices
Can shoppers distinguish between a lower total, better price per unit, and a lower-priced private-label option?
Trust and control
Do shoppers understand what changed and remain comfortable because every replacement is confirmed and reversible?
Next step
The next step is to validate whether shoppers need this support and whether the comparison flow reduces the effort of finding alternatives manually.

If shoppers understand and trust the recommendations, the next extension would let them save the optimized cart as a reusable Optimized Basket.

Repeated use could then provide evidence about shopping frequency, category spending, and which replacements shoppers accept or reverse. That evidence could help shape a future Budget-First Shopping experience with total and category spending limits.
